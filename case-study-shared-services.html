One Dispatch Centre for Police and Fire: A Shared Services Plan for a City and Its Police Service

Client
A Northern Ontario city and its police services board
Sector and location
Municipal government and policing, Ontario
Size
A police service of 220 employees
Services
Shared services feasibility study, service inventory, process and effort analysis, dispatch modelling, implementation planning
Timeframe
November 2023 to April 2024
Led by
Bruce Peever, FCMC

A city and its police services board wanted to ease pressure on taxes without cutting service. Bruce Peever reviewed how the two organizations deliver finance, human resources, procurement and dispatch. The review recommended formalizing how the City supports the Police Service, freeing the Police Service’s managers from transactional work, and moving fire dispatch into the police dispatch centre. In 2025, the City and the Police Services Board put the core of that advice into a three-year memorandum of understanding. That agreement also calls on the two organizations to look at sharing dispatch.

The Challenge

The City and its Police Services Board needed to reduce pressure on taxes while keeping or improving service levels. The Police Service, with 220 employees, relied on small finance and human resources teams whose staff held broader, more generalized roles than their City counterparts. The two organizations already worked together, but none of that collaboration was written down.

Dispatch added urgency. A Canadian Radio-television and Telecommunications Commission order to implement Next Generation 911 meant the Fire Department would need a new dispatch system, while the Police Service’s system already complied. The Fire Department also staffed dispatch with one firefighter per shift, typically a cadet, which left one fewer firefighter available for active duty on every shift.

The original scope included the local Central Ambulance Communications Centre as a possible dispatch partner. Early in the project, the City and Police Service decided it was not a suitable partner, and the dispatch work turned to a shared police and fire function.

The Approach

The review ran in four phases.

  1. Initiate Kickoff, project charter and stakeholder engagement plan
  2. Inventory Service inventory and current-state analysis
  3. Identify Shared service opportunities
  4. Recommend Target operating model and final report
  1. Start from the evidence. We reviewed organizational charts, financial reports, dispatch and call volume data and performance reports from the City, the Police Service and the Fire Department.
  2. Hear from the people doing the work. We held interviews and working sessions with 13 stakeholders from the three organizations to validate the current state and test each opportunity.
  3. Compare like with like. We built a service inventory and compared policies, processes, staffing, systems, volumes and cost per full-time employee across the organizations.
  4. Look at where the effort goes. We analysed how the Police Service Finance Manager spends their time, and reviewed two years of Fire Department call volumes and durations (2022 and 2023).
  5. Design the future and rank the options. For each opportunity we developed a target operating model, implementation challenges and estimated resources, costs and savings, then ranked every recommendation for complexity and impact before presenting the final report to senior leadership.

The Results

The City and Police Service received a ranked plan of eight core recommendations and five additional opportunities, each assessed for complexity, impact and timeline.

8
Core recommendations across finance, human resources, procurement and dispatch
4
Quick wins that need minimal resources and can start in the short term
4
Strategic projects, led by one shared police and fire dispatch centre

One dispatch centre for police and fire

  • Fire dispatch needs less than one full-time dispatcher. The Fire Department averaged about 2,420 calls a year in 2022 and 2023, with an average call lasting about 30 minutes. A fire dispatcher would have an estimated 40% to 50% of their time free to support police calls.
  • The Police Service already handles far higher volumes. It managed an average of 36,324 calls a year, some spanning weeks, on a system that already meets the Next Generation 911 order.
  • Four dispatchers per shift instead of five. A combined centre would need four dispatchers per shift, against five for separate police and fire dispatch, and would support the separate call-taker and dispatcher roles that simultaneous dispatch requires.
  • Firefighters back on active duty. Moving dispatch to the Police Service ends the practice of taking a firefighter off the line each shift, and the two organizations would share the cost of dispatchers, training and a compliant system.

Finance, human resources and procurement

  • Quick wins: formal, regular communication between the City and Police Service finance teams and between their human resources teams, and service level agreements and a memorandum of understanding that set out roles and expectations.
  • Higher-value work for the Police Service managers: the Finance Manager was spending about 60% of their effort on transactional and operational tasks. Delegating payroll and scheduling tasks would free a fifth to a quarter of their time for planning, reporting and advice. Staff self-service would do the same for the Human Resources Manager.
  • Procurement support: City resources and advice on the Police Service’s high-value purchases, to reduce risk and make procurement more effective.
  • Further opportunities: automating Police Service bidding through the City’s digital procurement software, more finance training for the Police Service’s senior team and Board, more use of human resources information systems, and offering dispatch services to smaller municipalities once police and fire dispatch are combined.

What happened next

In August 2025, the Police Services Board and the City approved a three-year memorandum of understanding that turns the review’s quick wins and support recommendations into commitments:

  • Regular contact: City finance and human resources staff meet their Police Service counterparts every quarter to coordinate budgeting, financial reporting and estimates, and to share practices, policy and strategies.
  • Defined City services: the memorandum sets out the finance and human resources services the City provides, including payroll processing, invoice payment, pension and retiree benefit administration and workplace insurance costs, along with the financial and budgeting system the Police Service uses.
  • Procurement support: City procurement staff give advice, best practices and templates on request, and review the Police Service’s high-risk procurements.
  • Clear accountability: the Police Services Board reports to Council every quarter, with performance metrics and a budget forecast, and provides a year-end financial report.
  • Dispatch: the memorandum calls on the City and Police Service to look for ways to share dispatch services.

Lessons for Other Municipalities

  • Let a mandated change open the door A required system upgrade is the moment to ask whether two services should share one system and one team.
  • Measure the workload before you merge Call volumes and durations showed that fire dispatch did not need a full position, which made the case for combining.
  • Write down the collaboration you already have Service level agreements and regular meetings are quick, low-cost wins that build trust for larger changes.
