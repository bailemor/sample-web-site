Public Ownership Lenders Can Trust, With Growers at the Table: A Governance Model for a Regional Irrigation System

Client
A regional municipality in one of Ontario’s specialty crop areas
Sector
Municipal government and agriculture
Services
Governance model assessment, jurisdictional review, financing-readiness analysis, Steering Committee facilitation, implementation roadmap
Timeframe
July to October 2026
Led by
Bruce Peever, FCMC

A regional municipality and its partners needed to choose who would own, finance and run a proposed communal irrigation system for local farms. Bruce Peever compared the options against what infrastructure lenders require and what growers need. The project’s Steering Committee chose a municipal services corporation with formal grower participation, and received a step-by-step roadmap to set it up.

The Challenge

The region is one of Canada’s most significant specialty crop areas, producing the majority of Ontario’s grapes and tender fruit on about 218,000 acres of farmland and more than 1,800 farms. Its grape, tender fruit, greenhouse and nursery sectors depend on reliable water. Drought and climate variability are increasing that need, yet irrigation access is uneven: some growers draw on municipal, private or farmer-led systems, while others have limited or unreliable supply. Building a private system is costly for an individual farm.

The proposed communal piped system would serve agricultural land between two of the region’s lower-tier municipalities. Studies in 2007, 2021 and 2022, including research with professors from Toronto Metropolitan University and Brock University, had confirmed the need for shared irrigation and explored governance options, but none had settled on a model. The project was now moving from feasibility and design into implementation planning, with two options left on the table: a municipal services corporation (MSC) or a grower co-operative.

The choice carried weight. The piped system would need significant long-term financing, potentially from the Canada Infrastructure Bank, and lenders look for a credible public sponsor, clear ownership and an enforceable way to collect user payments. Growers, who would use and pay for the system, also needed a real say in how it runs.

The Approach

The engagement ran in three phases from July to October 2026.

  1. Initiate July
  2. Compare July to September
  3. Roadmap September to October
  1. Build on earlier work. We reviewed the previous governance research, feasibility work and stakeholder engagement so the assessment started where those studies ended.
  2. Set out the legal options. We described how each model would be created and governed: an MSC under section 203 of Ontario’s Municipal Act, 2001 and Ontario Regulation 599/06, and a co-operative under the Co-operative Corporations Act.
  3. Compare three models. We assessed an MSC-only model, a grower co-operative and a hybrid in which an MSC owns the system and a co-operative operates it, on ownership, decision-making, accountability, operations and risk.
  4. Test lender readiness. We scored each model on what the Canada Infrastructure Bank and similar lenders look for: revenue security, sponsor credibility, repayment enforcement, user commitment and clear ownership.
  5. Learn from Ontario peers. We reviewed six Ontario comparators: three municipal services corporations (Union Water Supply System, Northern Maple Utilities and Frontenac Municipal Services) and three agricultural co-operatives (AGRIS Co-operative, Leamington Area Drip Irrigation and a long-established fruit growers’ co-operative). A scorecard rated each against lender requirements and user participation.
  6. Support the decision. We presented the findings to the Steering Committee and framed the choice around five practical questions: accountability, financial sustainability, grower participation, day-to-day operations and future growth.
  7. Plan the build. For the chosen model, we mapped six governance design decisions to the creation steps, roles, timelines, risks and mitigations needed to put each one in place.

The Results

The Steering Committee favoured an MSC-led governance and delivery model with formally embedded grower participation, and received a roadmap to establish it.

Why an MSC-led model

  • Lender confidence. The MSC’s statutory framework gives clear municipal accountability, defined ownership and public oversight, and municipal tools can support collection of unpaid charges. Co-operatives rely mainly on contracts with members.
  • Growers keep a voice without owning the system. The comparators showed the value of direct grower involvement. The MSC model embeds it through a Grower Council and, subject to shareholder approval, grower representation on the Board.
  • Less complexity than a hybrid. A hybrid would need two corporations and carries a risk of blurred roles. The roadmap adds a separate co-operative only if future operations show its benefits outweigh that complexity.

What the roadmap sets out

  • A shareholder agreement defining municipal ownership, voting thresholds and reserved matters, such as mandate changes, major financial commitments and dissolution.
  • A skills-based Board that combines municipal representatives with independent directors in finance, law, infrastructure and agriculture, with staggered terms.
  • A Grower Council with defined rights on rates, water allocation, service standards, drought protocols and capital priorities, written into the by-laws.
  • Binding connection and water-service agreements, and a user-fee framework of connection or capacity charges, fixed charges and volumetric water-use charges.
  • A choice of operating model (internal staff, municipal support or a contracted operator) and a day-one readiness checklist.
  • Five implementation risks with mitigations: municipal alignment, grower buy-in, role clarity, grower demand and revenue certainty, and water supply and climate.

Lessons for Other Municipalities

  • Choose governance with the lender in mind A model that works for day-to-day operations can still fail the tests a lender applies to sponsor strength, ownership and repayment.
  • Ownership and voice are separate questions Users can shape rates, allocation and service standards through formal rights without owning the corporation.
  • Secure demand before you build Confirm binding user commitments before finalizing capital and financing plans.
  • Add structure only when it earns its place Every additional entity adds agreements, roles and room for confusion.
